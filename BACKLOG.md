# Convergence for Emergence — Project Backlog

Derived from `Framework Critique.md` and the `Next Steps` sections across framework documents. Items are grouped by theme and roughly ordered by dependency.

---

## 🏗️ Framework Foundations

### [1] Remove unrealistic implementation timelines
The current framework specifies fixed-duration phases (e.g., "2–3 months", "6–12 months"). These need to be replaced with guidance that acknowledges transformation pace varies significantly by organization size, culture, and starting maturity. Reference: DevOps and FinOps adoption data shows multi-year real-world timelines even in high-readiness organizations.

### [2] Articulate ROI and cost-of-inaction narrative
Decision-makers need a financial lens. Document the quantifiable costs of siloed operations (rework, missed opportunities, governance failures) and the incremental value unlocked at each maturity level. Include guidance for resource-constrained environments on minimum viable implementation.

### [3] Define minimum viable starting points
Not all organizations can begin at the same entry point. Identify 2–3 concrete "quick win" convergence opportunities that deliver tangible benefits within the first 90 days regardless of current maturity level.

---

## 📏 Measurement & Feedback

### [4] Operationalize abstract metrics
Metrics like "Collaboration Index," "Knowledge Flow," and "Boundary Permeability" are directionally useful but not yet measurable. Define concrete data collection methods, scoring rubrics, and baseline-setting processes for each metric.

### [5] Create clear outcome attribution methods
Develop a methodology for attributing business outcomes (revenue, cost savings, risk reduction) specifically to convergence activities rather than other concurrent initiatives.

### [6] Build maturity reassessment cadence and feedback loops
Specify how often organizations should reassess maturity level, what triggers an unplanned reassessment, and how feedback from metric collection should inform framework iteration.

---

## 👥 People, Culture & Governance

### [7] Develop culture sections for each maturity level
Each maturity level currently describes structural characteristics but not cultural ones. Add descriptions of the prevailing mindset, resistance patterns, and cultural signals visible at Levels 1–5.

### [8] Address domain specialist evolution
Specialists fear loss of identity and depth in convergence models. Document explicitly how expertise is preserved and elevated — how specialists become involved in strategic decisions rather than replaced by generalists.

### [9] Define competency pathways by role
Identify the specific skills required at each maturity level for Convergence Facilitators, Domain Translators, Systems Architects, and Executive Sponsors. Create role-specific learning roadmaps from specialist to systems thinker.

### [10] Clarify governance hierarchy and decision authority
Current framework leaves ambiguous where convergence roles sit organizationally. Define reporting structures, decision-making authority, escalation paths, and conflict resolution processes at each maturity level.

---

## 🔧 Technical Implementation

### [11] Expand tooling and integration guidance
Provide concrete guidance on the technical architecture required to support each maturity level — including data alignment across systems, integration patterns, and tooling evolution from Level 1 through Level 5.

### [12] Address standardization vs. flexibility tension
Create guidance for organizations navigating existing technical constraints (regulatory, architectural, legacy). Frame constraints as documented guardrails rather than blockers, with patterns for innovating within them.

---

## 🌍 External Factors & Applicability

### [13] Develop observable symptom lists for each maturity level
Create a list of customer-facing and market-visible symptoms that should improve as organizations progress through maturity levels. This makes the framework tangible to executives and customers alike.

### [14] Address cross-cultural and global applicability
The current framework assumes a Western, individualistic organizational culture. Add guidance for collective-oriented cultures, multi-regional regulatory environments, and international implementation variations.

---

## 📚 Case Studies & Ecosystem

### [15] Develop initial case studies
Using the existing case study template, document 2–3 real or composite case studies covering different organization sizes and industries. Include failures and recovery — not just successes.

### [16] Map alignment with existing frameworks
Analyze C4E against ITIL, COBIT, SAFe, and other established methodologies. Identify gaps C4E fills, areas of overlap, and integration hooks so practitioners can adopt C4E alongside existing investments.

### [17] Scale guidance for organization size
Clarify how roles, responsibilities, and timelines scale across small (<500 employees), mid-market (500–5,000), and enterprise (5,000+) organizations.

## 🛡️ Governance Lenses — AI Service Adoption

### [25] Write integrations files for Compliance, Legal, and FinOps lenses
New lenses need corresponding integrations files following the pattern established by ethics-integrations.md and hr-training-integrations.md.

### [26] Update HR & Training lens to reflect AI service accountability framing
The HR lens governs the biological workforce. Add explicit treatment of how HR embeds AI service accountability into role definitions, performance criteria, and practitioner development — the human-side complement to Compliance/Legal governance of the instruments themselves.

### [27] Create cross-lens governance overview
Compliance, Legal, HR, and FinOps operate as a system around AI service governance. A short overview explaining how they interact — and why accountability for AI service behavior always lands on human practitioners — would help practitioners understand the model as a whole. This is also where cross-domain risk aggregation belongs: as an emergent property of lens integration at Level 3–4, not a standalone function.

### [28] Build Security lens
Security is a named core domain with established external frameworks (NIST CSF, CIS Controls, SLSA — already referenced in C4E onboarding). Lens should map C4E levels to those frameworks rather than duplicate them, and address AI service security governance as a distinct thread.

## ⚖️ Ethics Integration

### [18] Develop ethics governance as "conscience of the company"
Position the ethics component as an externalization of leadership values and a waste-reduction mechanism — not a prescriptive cultural overlay. Research integration points with existing ethics frameworks (IEEE Ethically Aligned Design, B Corp standards, etc.).

### [19] Create transparency and decision-making guardrail mechanisms
Define what transparency looks like at each maturity level and how ethics governance creates clearer, auditable decision-making trails. Develop this after leadership evolution (Backlog item #10) is established.

---

## 🛠️ Tooling & Community

### [20] Build maturity self-assessment tool
Create an interactive assessment instrument (web or worksheet) that organizations can use to score themselves against the maturity model and receive prioritized next-step recommendations.

### [21] Create visualization tools for domain convergence mapping
Develop templates or tooling for mapping current-state domain boundaries and interactions — a prerequisite for Phase 1 Assessment work.

### [22] Establish practitioner community
Define the structure and launch plan for a practitioner community where organizations can share convergence case studies, implementation challenges, and emergent methodologies.

### [23] Develop domain-specific implementation playbooks
Create supplementary guides for the six key domains (FinOps, Security & Compliance, Technology Delivery, Data Management, Ethical Technology, Business Strategy) that provide domain-specific convergence guidance.

---

## 📖 Training & Enablement

### [24] Create systems thinking curriculum
Develop a training program for building convergence skills at each organizational level — from individual contributors to executive sponsors. Include both conceptual foundation and applied workshop formats.
